📖 Reading Passage 1 — Academic Text

The Microbiome Revolution: How Gut Bacteria Shape Human Health

AInside the human gastrointestinal tract lives an extraordinarily complex community of microorganisms collectively known as the gut microbiome. Comprising more than 100 trillion individual bacteria, viruses, fungi, and archaea, this ecosystem contains approximately 150 times more genes than the human genome itself. Far from being passive passengers, these microbes engage in a sophisticated, bidirectional relationship with their host, influencing not merely digestion but an astonishing range of physiological and psychological processes.

BFor much of medical history, bacteria were viewed primarily through the lens of disease. The germ theory of the 19th century, while revolutionary in its time, inadvertently fostered a reductionist view of microorganisms as pathogens to be eliminated. Antibiotics — arguably the most transformative medical innovation of the 20th century — were deployed widely and often indiscriminately. Researchers now recognise that this approach came with a hidden cost: the disruption of the microbial communities that have co-evolved with humans over millions of years.

CThe modern microbiome field was catalysed by the Human Microbiome Project, a landmark initiative launched by the United States National Institutes of Health in 2007. The project aimed to characterise the microbial communities residing in and on the human body and to examine their role in human health and disease. What emerged was a portrait of enormous complexity and remarkable individuality — no two people share exactly the same microbial composition, even identical twins raised in the same household.

DAmong the most striking findings is the so-called gut-brain axis: a bidirectional communication network linking the central nervous system with the enteric nervous system of the gastrointestinal tract. The vagus nerve serves as a primary highway for this communication, transmitting signals in both directions. Studies have found that germ-free mice — animals raised in sterile conditions with no gut bacteria — display markedly abnormal behaviours including heightened anxiety and impaired social interaction. When the microbiomes of anxious mice were transplanted into relaxed mice, the recipients began exhibiting anxiety-like behaviours, strongly implicating the microbiome in regulating mood and temperament.

EThe clinical implications of this research are only beginning to be explored. Dysbiosis — an imbalance in the gut microbial community — has been associated with conditions as varied as inflammatory bowel disease, type 2 diabetes, obesity, rheumatoid arthritis, and depression. Faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), in which stool from a healthy donor is transferred to a recipient, has already proven highly effective for treating recurrent Clostridioides difficile infections. Researchers are now investigating FMT as a potential treatment for conditions including Parkinson's disease and autism spectrum disorder, though the evidence remains preliminary.

FDiet is widely acknowledged as the most powerful modifiable factor influencing the microbiome. A diet rich in diverse plant foods — vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, and fermented foods — tends to support a diverse and resilient microbial community. Conversely, diets high in processed foods, refined sugar, and saturated fat have been linked to reduced microbial diversity, a state increasingly associated with poor health outcomes. The Mediterranean diet, in particular, has attracted attention for its apparent ability to foster a favourable microbial profile.

GNevertheless, significant caution is warranted in this field. The microbiome is extraordinarily sensitive to methodological variation, making cross-study comparisons difficult. Many findings have emerged from animal studies that do not straightforwardly translate to humans. Furthermore, correlation does not imply causation: it remains unclear in many cases whether microbial imbalance causes disease or is a downstream consequence of it. As microbiologist Dr. Justin Sonnenburg has noted, "We are still in the early days of a science that has the potential to transform medicine — but we must resist the temptation to overstate what we currently know."
